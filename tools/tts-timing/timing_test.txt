"Wait!" she cried. "Don't go, not yet, not like this!"
He stopped. He turned. He looked at her, at the rain, at the door behind her, and then, very slowly, back at her face.
"Why?" he asked. "Why now, after all these years, after everything you said?"
Silence.
Well, there it was: the question she had been dreading since Tuesday; the one question she could not answer.
"I... I don't know," she whispered. "I thought — no, I hoped — that you would understand."
Dr. Hale, who had been listening from the hallway (as he always did), cleared his throat. "Forgive me, Mr. Graves, but the train leaves at 6:45, and it's already half past five."
Oh! Of course. The train.
She laughed, a short, bitter laugh, and wiped her eyes. "You see? Even the trains are against us!"
"Are they?" Graves said. "Or are we simply late, as usual, for everything?"
Nobody answered. Outside, a dog barked twice; somewhere a door slammed; the clock on the mantelpiece ticked on and on and on.
She had packed three things: a coat, a letter, and a photograph of her mother, faded, creased, and torn at one corner.
"No," she said at last. "No, no, no. I won't let you do this. Do you hear me? I won't!"
"Then come with me."
It was such a simple sentence, so quiet and so plain, that for a moment neither of them was sure it had been spoken at all.
Yes? No? Maybe? She couldn't tell anymore...
The long grey road that wound between the hills toward the coast had never seemed so empty or so far away as it did that evening.
"I—I can't," she said. "But I will. Tomorrow. I promise."
